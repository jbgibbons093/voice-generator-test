We wanted to understand how generic and brand-name medicines contribute to patients’ out-of-pocket costs, so we analyzed a nationally representative survey of prescription use and spending in twenty twenty-two. More than half of Americans filled a generic prescription, compared with about one in six who filled a brand-name prescription. Although brand-name drugs were more expensive per medicine, generics accounted for fifty-five percent of total out-of-pocket prescription spending. Our findings suggest that policies to reduce patients’ costs should address both. Lower brand-name prices could reduce what patients pay through deductibles and coinsurance. For generics, we highlight changes in insurance benefits: limiting out-of-pocket costs for high-value generic medicines, preventing patient charges from exceeding cash prices, or eliminating generic copayments altogether. These approaches address both the prices of medicines and how insurance determines patients’ costs. You can read our full study in JAMA Network Open.
